THE REALITY
An emergency is anything routine work cannot absorb.

An emergency is any event, sudden or slow, that seriously disrupts a community or a country and exceeds what its institutions can handle with their routine work. Most begin at home; some arrive from across a border. Every country has to be equipped for its own emergencies and for what is coming its way from beyond its borders.

The emergencies nobody declares

A flood announces itself. A drought builds for months before anyone declares it. A contaminated product spreads before the first illness is linked to it. A programme ends on a date, and the services it carried end with it. No siren sounds and no operations centre opens. These are emergencies all the same, measured in the same lives. They need the same thing as a flood: institutions already working together, named, qualified and measured, before anyone calls it an emergency.

What arrives
Sudden.

Maiduguri, 2024: a dam gave way at night and submerged 40 per cent of the city. More than 400,000 people moved into camps and settlements, the teaching hospital went under water, the zoo's surviving animals escaped into the streets, and floodwater carried the risk of cholera into crowded sites.

Slow.

Southern Africa, 2024: drought left 68 million people in need of aid, 17 per cent of the region. Six countries declared national disasters as harvests failed and livestock died.

Across borders.

A locust front, a transboundary animal disease or a shared river reaches the next country on its own schedule, not after neighbours have compared notes.

Contaminated.

South Africa, 2017 to 2018: clinicians flagged an unusual number of infections in newborns in July 2017. The source, a processed meat factory, was named in March 2018. By the end, 1,060 people had been confirmed ill and 216 had died, in the largest listeriosis event on record. Food safety authorities carry the investigation; this is the point where it outgrew routine work.

When the funding stops.

A study of 93 low- and middle-income countries projects that current cuts in development assistance could cost 9.4 million lives by 2030, 2.5 million of them children under five. The clinics, the testing and the early warning end with the programme.

Alongside all of these, disease events never pause. The WHO African Region records more than 120 public health emergencies a year, and cholera reached 24 countries in 2025.

What Africa has built

National disaster management and civil protection authorities, with district disaster committees. Public health emergency operations centres. Veterinary services, plant protection units, and environmental regulators with accredited laboratories.

An IHR National Focal Point in every country. Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response down to the district. Surge teams trained to deploy within days. Each depends on a position someone holds today.

Much of it arrived through funded programmes, and every item is perishable. Procedures age from the day they are signed. Trained officers transfer. Surge rosters go stale between exercises. An operations centre is staffed by people who inherit a desk rather than a position with a history.

Every institution does its job. What no institution has been assigned is keeping the response standing between events.

THE COST
Emergency response is judged on time. The time is lost between institutions.

The Joint External Evaluation scores emergency response operations. The annual self-assessment scores event management. Many countries track the 7-1-7 target: detect within seven days, notify within one, complete the early response within seven. The delays rarely sit inside one institution. They sit at the handoffs: notification waits on knowing who to call, confirmation waits on finding a laboratory that runs the method, and response waits on assembling a team.

THE CHANNELS
Five mandates, one event. Each depends on positions someone holds today.
Disaster Management

Disaster committees, early warning and early action, evacuation, shelter, logistics and stockpiles, and the partners who arrive with support.

When the district committee activates, does it reach health, water and veterinary services by name, and does anyone confirm they received it?

Health Emergencies

Operations centres, incident management, rapid response teams, surveillance and response, the IHR focal point, and the laboratories that confirm.

Who holds the incident manager position today, and is the rapid response roster the one you approved, or the one from the last event?

Animal and Plant Health

Transboundary animal disease, movement control, carcass disposal, international notification, and locust, armyworm and quarantine pests.

Who is named to act on a finding in the herd this week, and who tells the border before the animals or the grain move?

Environment, Chemicals and Water

Contaminated water, chemical releases, mining and industrial discharge, and the accredited environmental laboratories that confirm them.

Which laboratory can confirm the contaminant, is its proficiency current for that method, and does the result reach health while the exposure can still be stopped?

Food, Nutrition and Social Protection

Food safety authorities, food crisis early warning, nutrition treatment, and cash and food transfers. Routine investigation, recall and seasonal targeting stay with their own authorities. What belongs here is the moment one of them outgrows routine work, and who is already on the team when it does.

When the warning says the lean season will be severe, which positions in agriculture, health and social protection confirm they received it, and what did each decide?

Counterpart positions across the border are part of being ready, not an extra.

WHY THEY ARE ONE SYSTEM
One event, several operations centres. Each sees what the others need.

Disaster management knows where people have moved. Health knows where illness is appearing. Veterinary services know which herds are sick. The environmental regulator knows which water is unsafe. Agriculture knows which harvest failed. None is another’s early warning system. Each sees what the others cannot.

A contaminated product does not announce whether it was an accident, fraud or a deliberate act, and in the first days nobody knows which. The response needs the same people either way, with security services working as a peer.

What fails is rarely the capacity. It is the step after the first finding: someone deciding to make a call, knowing who to call, and that person still holding the position. That pathway has to exist before the event.

WHAT COLLABORATION REQUIRES
A response team is a standing condition, not a meeting called on the day.

A committee, a phone list, a plan signed years ago: all of it is real, and none of it is a team on the morning an event begins. The operational sense is narrower and harder. Several institutions working the same event, each bringing what only they can see, under the same plan, while each keeps its own mandate entirely.

01
The team exists before the event.

One cross-domain structure, with roles defined, positions named and current, alternates named and people trained. When a position falls vacant, the system knows the same day.

02
Everyone works from the same plan.

One current version of each response plan and procedure, owned, dated and acknowledged by every position assigned to it.

03
Everyone hears at once, and it can be shown.

Warnings and activations reach positions in every authority by email, SMS and voice, with delivery confirmed for each recipient.

04
Every finding has an owner and closes with evidence.

A gap from an event or an exercise is assigned to a position, given a deadline, closed on uploaded evidence and retested in the next exercise.

Laboratory confirmation sits under all four.

A result is acted on when the laboratory behind it is trusted: proficiency current, accreditation in scope, analyst qualified. Each laboratory keeps its own quality system and its own authority. What changes is that the ministries relying on it can see why the result deserves to be trusted, before they act on it.

Many of the people in a response are not civil servants: the Red Cross, water utilities, transporters, private laboratories and humanitarian partners. They take part in assigned roles, without access to any national system.

When those four hold, activation is a step rather than a scramble.

THE MISSING PIECE
What is missing is infrastructure for the team between events.

Response authorities have built strong capacity inside each mandate. The work between them still depends on a committee, a phone list and the people who happen to know one another.

No authority can reasonably build that space alone. Whoever builds it funds it, and the others plan around a commitment they do not control. Surge funding is scoped to an event. A programme has a host and an end date.

The OACI Platform gives that space a standing collaboration layer. It is not an operations centre, a surveillance system or a logistics system, and it replaces none of them. National systems do the sensing. OACI makes sure the finding reaches the positions that act on it, together, and that the proof of what happened remains. The operations centre stays. What OACI holds is the standing layer between centres, so the team exists before the event and still exists when the grant that trained it has closed.

When the funding ends, the team does not.

The roster, the qualifications, the plans and the record of every fix are bound to positions on infrastructure the country owns.

PROVEN AT NATIONAL SCALE
Standing response teams have already outlasted their funding.

The model began with food emergencies in the United States, where investigators from different agencies were running parallel, separate investigations of the same event. Standing multi-agency teams were built to end that: members named in advance by position, one plan held in one place, a command arrangement agreed before any event, and joint exercises. Each team starts from a capability assessment that names its gaps. After every incident or exercise, an after-action report follows and every action item is tracked until it closes. Defined from the start as all-hazards, thirty teams work this way today on the same collaboration technology OACI brings to Africa. Some operate with no dedicated funding and still exercise, measure and close their findings. That is the evidence that the team outlasts the grant.

CAPABILITIES
Eight conditions carry the response, whatever the hazard turns out to be.

Response fails in the same few ways whether the trigger is a flood, a fever, contaminated food or a failed rain, and each is visible long before anything happens.

THE STANDING RESPONSE TEAM
When the event is confirmed, is a team stood up, or assembled?

Multi-agency teams drawn from health, disaster management, animal and plant health, environment and food, named in advance by position, trained together and exercised on the procedures they will use for real. The team works under a command arrangement agreed before the event, or the one the country already uses. The event decides which domains activate, on agreed triggers, not who heard first. For emergencies that build slowly, warning-to-decision pathways are exercised before the season, every step time-stamped and every decision assigned to a position. Every activation or exercise ends in an after-action report, each action with an owner and a deadline, retested in the next exercise.

DELIVERY CONFIRMED
Did the activation reach everyone?

Warnings and activations reach positions in every authority by email, SMS and voice, with delivery confirmed for each recipient. An alert nobody answers escalates on a timer.

A CURRENT DIRECTORY
Who holds the district disaster position today?

One directory of positions across every response authority, each with a named owner and a named alternate. Filled against vacant is a live figure rather than an organogram.

LABORATORY CONFIRMATION
Which laboratory can confirm it, and will the next authority act on the result?

Proficiency, accreditation scope, equipment and analyst certification tracked across clinical, veterinary, food and environmental laboratories. Each laboratory keeps its own quality system and its own authority.

ONE CURRENT VERSION
Is every district using the response plan you approved?

Response plans and procedures live in one shared workspace with a version, a named owner, a review date and an audit trail on every change, acknowledged by every position assigned to them.

COMPETENCE RECORDS
Is the incident manager still qualified?

Certificates issued, tracked and renewed, with training assigned when a gap calls for it, wherever the training is delivered. Records export in the formats audit and accreditation accept.

CLOSURE ON EVIDENCE
Which of last year’s after-action findings actually closed, and who closed them?

Every finding from an event or an exercise is assigned to a position, given a deadline that escalates when it passes, and closed only on uploaded evidence. The next exercise tests whether the fix held.

CONTINUOUS READINESS
If asked today how ready the country is to respond, where does the answer come from?

Readiness scored against targets the country sets for itself, each figure dated. Exercises run on a calendar, and every exercise finding enters the same closure cycle as a live one.

RECORDS THAT STAY
When the incident manager transfers, what does the successor inherit?

Plans, counterparts, decisions and open findings bound to the position, not the person, so the team survives turnover and the end of a programme.

HOW A COUNTRY BEGINS
Start with the emergency you already know is coming.

Flood season. A programme ending next year. Cholera season. The lean season. Any of these can anchor the first standing team.

Deeper.

The same team structure from national to province and district. The same directory, plans and closure cycle, applied where the work actually happens.

Wider.

The team a country builds for its first entry point is the team every other solution activates, with new domains joining as their positions are filled.

Across.

Counterpart positions with neighbours for what travels: a river basin, a locust front, animal disease, a health event.

What a country commits to at the start is small: a named position accountable for the roster, a first response plan and a date for the first exercise. The first year is a measurement, not a verdict.

ADDRESSED TO HEADS OF EMERGENCY OPERATIONS, DISASTER MANAGEMENT AND IHR NATIONAL FOCAL POINTS
When the river rose or the rains failed, a community never answered one household at a time. It answered as one.

What an emergency authority gains is the part of its work that depends on other institutions. The Joint External Evaluation, the annual self-assessment, the International Health Regulations and the national disaster plan all assume other institutions will act on its activation. That is the work OACI holds. In the first year the test is practical: a roster named and current, one plan exercised, after-action findings closed on evidence. The layer outlasts the surge budget.

OACI gives the institutions that answer now one standing team for whatever comes. Each keeps its own mandate, its own systems and its own budget. A briefing starts with where the last response lost time.
