Africa knew One Health before it had a name. Now it is reclaiming that heritage at the scale of a continent. Fifty-five nations. Four health domains. One shared operational layer, designed so that ministries, laboratories and agencies can work as one, before the next health event, not after.

A country should know whether its One Health system is ready before a health event tests it. OACI is built to make readiness something a country defines, measures and manages.

FRAMEWORK & APPROACH
What One Health depends on

Communities across this continent have long understood people, animals, plants and land as one living system. A herder read the condition of the herd as a statement about the water, the grazing and the season, and knew that what moved through the animals would move through the household. A healer knew that the health of plants depended on the soil and the rain, and the health of the family on both. It was one body of knowledge, held together.

What has changed is that this knowing is now divided across ministries, each holding one part.

One Health works when a signal in one part of the system reaches every institution that needs to act on it, while there is still time to act. A threat detected in animals may require human health surveillance, laboratory confirmation, environmental investigation or action affecting food production. A crop pest or a contaminated consignment can move the other way, into trade, nutrition and human health. Where a signal starts is not necessarily where its consequences end.

Every country holds these mandates, at very different levels of capability. The challenge is the shared operating layer between them: keeping the picture of what is ready, what is missing and who owns the gap current. One Health depends on what happens between institutions: whether the signal reaches the right people, whether responsibilities are clear, whether procedures are current, whether the required capabilities are ready, and whether institutions can act together without building the relationships during the event.

The four domains matter because a signal in any one of them can require action from the others.

Human Health

Integrated surveillance and response systems connecting hospitals, clinics and community health workers across borders.

Animal Health

Veterinary services and livestock disease control that protect herds, livelihoods and the export markets that depend on them.

Plant Health

Crop surveillance, plant pest monitoring, and phytosanitary systems that protect food security and prevent cross-border disease transmission through agricultural trade.

Environmental Health

Climate, water and ecosystem monitoring that predicts health risks and builds resilience into national planning.

THE REALITY
Africa built a continental body for every health domain.
What is still missing is a standing way for a country’s own institutions, and its neighbours’, to work together directly, every day.

Each of the four health domains has its own continental body, and most are older than the term One Health. Animal health has the African Union Inter-African Bureau for Animal Resources (AU-IBAR), founded in Nairobi in 1951. Plant health has the Inter-African Phytosanitary Council, established in 1954. Environment has the African Ministerial Conference on the Environment (AMCEN), established in Cairo in 1985. Human health has the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC), the African Union’s public health agency. Health and environment ministers from 52 African nations came together at Libreville in 2008.

Two years later they met again in Luanda to accelerate what they had already agreed, and set themselves a schedule: the ministers would reconvene every two years to carry the work forward.

They next met eight years later. In 2018 they returned to Libreville and adopted a ten-year plan running to 2029. That plan is still in force. No conference has been recorded since.

The commitment held, meeting after meeting. What was missing was any way to carry out what the meetings agreed, between one meeting and the next.

Alongside these continental efforts, thirty-four countries in sub-Saharan Africa have hosted a National Bridging Workshop with the World Health Organization (WHO) and the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), assessing collaboration across sixteen technical areas and producing a national roadmap. Countries have written strategies, signed memoranda of understanding, deployed tools and stood up committees.

A roadmap still has to be carried after everyone leaves the room.

Every one of those is a record of a moment. A workshop is three days. A strategy has a date range. An instrument creates a committee. An assessment reports a point in time.

Countries already share good tools. District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2), developed on a not-for-profit basis and used across the continent, turns a country’s health data into reports and dashboards, and its use is being extended to animal health and One Health. What a dashboard shows still has to become a decision: who needs to see it, who decides, who acts, and whether the action was completed. That layer between the data and the decision is where One Health is won or lost, across every institution it touches, and it has to stay in place between events, funding cycles and postings.

The space between ministries stays empty because no one owns it. Each budget is tied to one mandate, and each grant ends. Whoever builds the connection has to host and fund it, and the others become guests in a system they do not control. Shared infrastructure fits that space: it is built once and its cost is shared by every institution that uses it, so none has to develop, run and sustain the connection on its own, or fund again what has already been built.

The gap is not commitment. Eighteen years of signatures and work demonstrate the commitment. The gap is making the operating state of collaboration visible, manageable and sustainable every day, not only during a crisis.

THE COST
One health event, held by two ministries.

Health events in Africa do not stay in one domain. A disease that passes from animals to people, a pest that destroys a harvest, a drought that turns into hunger, a contaminated food that closes a market: each is counted by a different institution, yet each reaches the others. Each also reaches trade, livelihoods and the economy, because the health of people, animals, plants and the land is what a country lives on.

Between 20 September and 30 October 2025, Rift Valley fever reached 404 confirmed human cases, including 42 deaths, in Mauritania and Senegal. In the same weeks veterinary services recorded 160 confirmed animal cases in Senegal and reported 62 outbreaks in animals in Mauritania. One event, held by two ministries in each country.

DEFINE. MEASURE. MANAGE. IMPROVE.
You cannot manage what you have not defined.
You cannot improve what you have not measured.

One Health becomes manageable when ready stops being an aspiration and becomes a defined condition. The country defines that condition. Current state can then be measured against it, gaps can be managed, and improvement can be demonstrated rather than assumed. This is a quality system applied to national coordination: a defined standard, objective evidence of conformance, competence records, corrective action carried to closure, and review between external evaluations.

The gap is the distance between what the country defined as ready and what is true today.

DEFINE

The country says what ready means. Not us.

Each country defines what One Health looks like for its own ministries: which domains connect, which ministry holds which role, which policies and Memoranda of Understanding (MOUs) must exist, which procedures, training, exercises and laboratory capabilities count as ready. Named, dated, owned.

Without a defined target, progress is a feeling. With one, “are we collaborating” becomes a question with a dated answer.

A focal point is the named officer who represents a ministry or institution in One Health work and is the person other institutions contact.

A country’s definition of ready will expose capability it does not yet have, not only connections it has not made. That is the point. A gap with a name, an owner and a date is a case the country can act on and a funder can invest in.

MEASURE

Current state against that target, continuously.

Readiness, here, means something simple: whether the people, procedures and laboratories a country needs to act together are in place today. Most countries see this only from time to time, through an evaluation such as the Joint External Evaluation (JEE) or the Performance of Veterinary Services (PVS) evaluation, which reports every few years. What happens between those evaluations is where One Health either works or does not. The officer who represents a ministry is posted elsewhere and the position sits empty. A joint procedure passes its review date and no one reviews it. A laboratory’s approval to run a test expires. A problem found in the last exercise is still not fixed. Each of these is known inside the institution it belongs to, and none of it sits in one place.

The readiness view is designed to show current state against the country’s own target and to track the gap over time, continuously. It is designed to show each of the things in the paragraph above, and whether the country’s coordination policies are signed or still drafts.

The public and industry interface is designed to be tracked the same way, so external collaboration is measured, not assumed.

MANAGE

A procedure in a binder is not a capability.

People move. Procedures age. Somebody has to keep the definition current. When either changes, the operating state of the system changes with it.

A procedure trained once, three years ago, when the people in the room have since moved to other posts, is not a capability either. The muscle memory is gone.

A veterinarian and an epidemiologist cannot work out how to work together while a health event is unfolding. That has to happen in a training room, more than once, until it is reflex. Cross-domain training builds the shared language that makes the first hour of a response productive instead of the first week.

A procedure that still names an officer who has left is out of date, whatever its version number says, and a focal point who transferred in March leaves a vacancy until someone fills the position. Both are designed to be tracked against the country’s own definition, so the readiness view changes the day a post turns vacant.

Training is designed to be scheduled against the roster, so every focal point knows what is current, what is due, and which finding from the last exercise is driving the next session. Not an event. A cycle.

IMPROVE

A gap found in an exercise is a problem solved.

An emergency is the worst possible time to find out which part of the plan does not work. A tabletop exercise reveals in four hours what a health event reveals in four weeks: the phone number that no longer works, the procedure that contradicts another ministry’s, the reporting line nobody defined, the threshold nobody agreed. Finding those in an exercise costs a room and a day. Finding them in an emergency costs lives and credibility.

Exercises are designed to run on a schedule across every connected domain. Each one produces findings with named owners and closure dates, and each finding stays visible in the readiness view until it closes. Strategic exercises test the design rather than the drill: whether the right ministries are in the room, whether the law supports the coordination the plan assumes, whether the money moves in hours instead of weeks.

An assessment identifies the gap. A strategy sets the direction. Neither one carries the work to closure or shows whether capability actually improved. That is what an operating system does, and it is the difference between a country that documents its intentions and a country that can demonstrate what changed.

THE MISSING PIECE
The country holds the institutions and the mandate. OACI is built to make the connections between them operable, visible and persistent.

OACI does not replace ministries, laboratories, surveillance systems or national authority, and it does not compete with the surveillance, laboratory and information systems a country already runs or that its partners already fund. It is built to provide the shared operating layer above them, through which the country can define readiness, see current state, manage the gaps and hold capability over time.

What OACI is designed to carry between institutions is the signal and the proof that it is fit to act on: which laboratory can run the method within the scope it is accredited for, who is qualified to sign the result, which procedure is current. Where capability is uneven, collaboration is what makes it usable across the country. A district that cannot confirm a result will have a route to one that can, and the standing to use it. It is built country first: a country’s institutions connect with each other, then with their neighbours’, directly, without every exchange having to pass through a continental or international body, while those bodies keep setting the direction each country carries out.

This connective layer is the glue: the part that makes existing institutions act as one. OACI is built to connect them. It does not replace them.

One Health is familiar here. OACI is built to give Africa the infrastructure to practise it at the scale of modern institutions, and to help lead it for the world.

The OACI Platform

The platform is not a screen people meet in front of. It is built to run the collaboration.

It is designed to hold the directory of every institution, role and focal point, with an owner accountable for each one being current. It is designed to stand up a secure event workgroup in minutes, with the right people already in it, because the roles are assigned before the event. It is designed to carry the Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), versioned and assigned, so roles are read rather than argued. It is built to run the training register and the exercise cycle. Training records and certificates are designed to stay with the people who earned them, which is what an accreditation body, a trading partner and a donor all ask for, and what opens cross-border work.

Every item on it is designed to have an owner and a date. That is the difference between a system that runs and a report that gets read.

Knowledge

Procedures, guidance, case definitions, training content, exercise reports, evaluations, agreements and the decisions taken in meetings are national capability only if the people who need them can find the current version. Too often that material sits on personal drives, in email and in workshop hard copy. OACI is designed to keep it permissioned, versioned and connected to the roles and the work that depend on it.

When people move, the knowledge is designed to stay.

Laboratories

A country’s laboratory capability is distributed across institutions and often across several ministries. That makes laboratories one of the clearest tests of whether One Health is actually connected. The country should be able to see which laboratory can perform which test, to which method and at which tier, within the scope it is accredited for. It should see whether proficiency results confirm the capability is there, and where a required capability does not yet exist. A country knows what it can test before it needs to test it, not during.

Standards and capability

Coordination is worth what the capability underneath it is worth. A country may be working toward ISO/IEC 17025 for testing laboratories, ISO 15189 for medical laboratories, or a food safety or inspection standard a trading partner recognises. Its requirements, evidence, competence records and open non-conformities are designed to be held and tracked continuously rather than assembled before an audit.

That is designed to turn accreditation from an event a laboratory prepares for into a state a country can see.

OACI is bringing laboratory design, audit and standards expertise alongside the platform, so a country closing a capability gap has both the system that tracks it and the people who have done the work.

Integration

OACI is built to connect through application programming interfaces (APIs) and other authorized interfaces to the national systems a country already operates. Those systems remain authoritative for the data and functions they own. The country decides which systems connect, which signals and indicators are exposed, what triggers collaboration, who may see what, and what flows back.

The signals, indicators and workflow triggers a country authorizes are designed to activate the collaboration around them, bringing the right institutions, roles, procedures and work teams together. Because OACI is designed to operate across institutional boundaries, it is designed to assemble an authorized cross-domain operating picture that no single ministry system is designed to provide.

Integration moves the signal. OACI is built to move the work.

As neighbouring countries join, they will join the same directory, the same methods and the same procedures, so the first cross-border event is not the first time anyone has met.

The result will be a national One Health operating picture without creating another institution or another command center to build, staff and fund.

Every nation keeps control of its own data and its own mandate. A country sends only what it chooses to send: the alerts, indicators, and workflow that coordination needs, never access to the underlying records. Access follows the country’s own permissions, roles and data sharing rules, set by the national data controller. Data sovereignty is a design property of the system, not an assurance offered alongside it.

The platform is built on Government-Standard Security: encryption to AES-256, multi-factor authentication, role-based access and continuous monitoring. The controls, where records reside and who operates the deployment are set in each country’s agreement.

Institutional memory is not a quality an organisation has. It is what remains when the roles, the procedures, the knowledge, the training record and the open findings are held in a system rather than in the people who happen to occupy the posts. When a ministry director changes, the next director inherits that record, not a request to start over.

The continental strategies call for work across mandates. This is the infrastructure that work depends on. Each country shapes it around its own One Health priorities, set by its geography, its current capabilities and its economy.

OACI is designed to support the frameworks of the African Union and Africa CDC, the continental zoonotic disease strategy, the regional One Health strategies, the Quadripartite’s One Health Joint Plan of Action, and each country’s own plan.

Government does not see every signal first, and it does not carry the response alone.

Industry often sees a problem in its own product, its own herds or its own supply chain before it reaches a surveillance system. Communities see what is happening where they live before it reaches a clinic. When the response comes, a recall runs through industry, a campaign runs through communities, and a trade decision is carried by exporters. A country that can only work with what its own ministries hold is working with part of the picture and part of the capacity.

Vetted industry, laboratory, academic and community participants can be admitted to a specific secure workgroup, for a specific event or policy question, under permissions set by the national data controller. They can contribute to the work.

Public alerts are designed to go out with delivery confirmed and a defined stand-down, not broadcast and assumed. What comes back from the field and the public arrives through structured intake that is acknowledged, triaged and routed to an action, so a report from a district or a citizen becomes work with an owner rather than a message someone read.

Collaboration will reach past the ministry wall.

Access to national records will not.

For more than twenty years, one collaboration infrastructure has connected the regulators, health officials, laboratories and response teams of United States federal agencies and all fifty states, across human health, animal health, food, the environment and emergency response, co-funded by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), through a university centre that brought further grants and programmes behind it.

OACI does not build Africa’s collaboration infrastructure from scratch. It is leveraging the infrastructure proven in the United States: building on it and adapting it to Africa’s own institutions, priorities and needs, as an African system, African-owned and African-governed.

No African country funds the build. What each country contributes goes to adapting and running OACI, and every country that joins lowers the cost for the others. OACI is designed so that what works in one country is carried into the next instead of started again. Development partners and funders can support a country’s use of OACI instead of commissioning another separate build: the funder keeps the credit, the country keeps the capability, and the investment compounds.

ONE HEALTH: THE ENTRY POINT
Every country enters One Health through the priorities its ministries already hold.

Countries do not have to build One Health all at once.

A country starts with work it already has in hand: the roadmap from its bridging workshop, the priority zoonosis in its strategy, or a map of who holds each One Health role. The infrastructure will work from the first day; nothing has to be built before the work starts.

One Health

Connect the ministries, define what ready means across all four domains, and see current state continuously.

Public Health

Human and animal health surveillance, response roles, procedures and laboratory confirmation working as one workflow.

Food Safety

Inspection and investigation workflows, establishment and facility registries, laboratory confirmation against method, recall and traceback coordination across ministries, and industry working inside the same secure workgroups.

Food Security

Production, market, climate and plant health signals reaching the institutions that can act while there is still time to act, rather than after a shortfall becomes an emergency.

Environmental Health

Water, air, waste and ecosystem signals connected to the human and animal health institutions that have to respond to them.

Laboratory Networks

Which laboratory runs which test, to which method, at which tier, with proficiency, referral and accreditation status visible across every ministry that depends on it.

Emergency Response

Rosters, procedures, workgroups, exercise cycles and corrective actions held ready before an event, so activation is a step rather than a scramble.

One Health in OACI will be delivered by Africans, for Africa: African experts who have done the work inside ministries of health, agriculture and environment, veterinary services, laboratories and regional bodies, and around them a wider network OACI is building of national institutions, universities, professional associations and champions in each country.
