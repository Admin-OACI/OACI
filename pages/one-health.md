Africa knew One Health before it had a name. Now it is reclaiming that heritage at the scale of a continent. Fifty-five nations. Four health domains. One shared operational layer through which ministries, laboratories and agencies work as one, before the next health event, not after.

A country should know whether its One Health system is ready before the next health event tests it. OACI makes readiness something a country defines, measures and manages.

FRAMEWORK & APPROACH
What One Health depends on

Communities across this continent have long understood people, animals, plants and land as one living system. A herder read the condition of the herd as a statement about the water, the grazing and the season, and knew that what moved through the animals would move through the household. A healer knew that the health of plants depended on the soil and the rain, and the health of the family on both. It was one body of knowledge, held together.

What has changed is that this knowing is now divided across ministries, each holding one part.

One Health works when a signal in one part of the system reaches every institution that needs to act on it, while there is still time to act. A threat detected in animals may require human health surveillance, laboratory confirmation, environmental investigation or action affecting food production. A crop pest or a contaminated consignment can move the other way, into trade, nutrition and human health. Where a signal starts is not necessarily where its consequences end.

Every country holds these mandates, at very different levels of capability. The challenge is the shared operating layer between them: keeping the picture of what is ready, what is missing and who owns the gap current. One Health depends on what happens between institutions: whether the signal reaches the right people, whether responsibilities are clear, whether procedures are current, whether the required capabilities are ready, and whether institutions can act together without building the relationships during the event.

The four domains matter because a signal in any one of them can require action from the others.

Human Health

Integrated surveillance and response systems connecting hospitals, clinics and community health workers across borders.

Animal Health

Veterinary services and livestock disease control that protect herds, livelihoods and the export markets that depend on them.

Plant Health

Crop surveillance, plant pest monitoring, and phytosanitary systems that protect food security and prevent cross-border disease transmission through agricultural trade.

Environmental Health

Climate, water and ecosystem monitoring that predicts health risks and builds resilience into national planning.

THE REALITY
Africa built a continental body for every health domain.
What was never built is the infrastructure between them.

Each of the four health domains has its own continental body, and most are older than the term One Health. Animal health has AU-IBAR, founded in Nairobi in 1951. Plant health has the Inter-African Phytosanitary Council, established in 1954. Environment has AMCEN, established in Cairo in 1985. Health and environment ministers from 52 African nations came together at Libreville in 2008, met again in Luanda in 2010, and in 2018 adopted a plan that runs to 2029.

Two years later they met again in Luanda to accelerate what they had already agreed, and set themselves a schedule: the ministers would reconvene every two years to carry the work forward.

They next met eight years later. In 2018 they returned to Libreville and adopted a ten-year plan running to 2029. That plan is still in force. No conference has been convened since.

The commitment held. What did not hold was anything that carried the work between the rooms where the commitment was made.

Alongside these continental efforts, thirty-four countries in sub-Saharan Africa have hosted a National Bridging Workshop with WHO and the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), assessing collaboration across sixteen technical areas and producing a national roadmap. Around twenty have created a One Health platform or coordination mechanism by ministerial or presidential instrument. Countries have written strategies, signed memoranda of understanding, deployed tools and stood up committees.

A roadmap still has to be carried after everyone leaves the room.

Every one of those is a record of a moment. A workshop is three days. A strategy has a date range. An instrument creates a committee. An assessment reports a point in time. The programme itself added a second phase and began placing national experts inside government to chase whether roadmap actions actually happen.

Infrastructure is different. It is what stays in place between events, funding cycles and postings: who holds which role, which procedures are in force, what the country can test, who is trained, what was exercised, and which agreed action is still open. It also holds the rules that govern all of it. Who is entitled to see what, in which role, under whose authority, for which event, for how long, and with a record of who saw it. A data sharing tool moves information between systems and belongs to whoever deployed it. It does not hold who holds each position, whether a procedure is current, or whether an action closed. It does not decide who is permitted to see what, because that is a policy decision no tool can make for a ministry. And it does not survive the project that funded it.

Beneath each ministry sit laboratories, surveillance programmes, inspectors, emergency operations, universities and field personnel. Every country has built some of this and is still building the rest. Collaboration depends on what happens between them: whether signals move, whether responsibilities are clear, whether procedures remain current, whether people are prepared, and whether identified gaps are actually closed.

The space between ministries stays empty because no one owns it. Each budget is tied to one mandate, and each grant ends. Whoever builds the connection has to host and fund it, and the others become guests in a system they do not control. Shared infrastructure, with its cost carried by every institution that uses it, fits that space.

The gap is not commitment. Eighteen years of signatures and work demonstrate the commitment. The gap is making the operating state of collaboration visible, manageable and sustainable every day, not only during a crisis.

DEFINE. MEASURE. MANAGE. IMPROVE.
You cannot manage what you have not defined.
You cannot improve what you have not measured.

One Health becomes manageable when ready stops being an aspiration and becomes a defined condition. The country defines that condition. Current state can then be measured against it, gaps can be managed, and improvement can be demonstrated rather than assumed. This is a quality system applied to national coordination: a defined standard, objective evidence of conformance, competence records, corrective action carried to closure, and review between external evaluations.

The gap is the distance between what the country defined as ready and what is true today.

DEFINE

The country says what ready means. Not us.

Each country defines what One Health looks like for its own ministries: which domains connect, which ministry holds which role, which policies and Memoranda of Understanding (MOUs) must exist, which procedures, training, exercises and laboratory capabilities count as ready. Named, dated, owned.

Without a defined target, progress is a feeling. With one, “are we collaborating” becomes a question with a dated answer.

A focal point is the named officer who represents a ministry or institution in One Health work and is the person other institutions contact.

This is not OACI’s definition of readiness. It is the country’s definition, made operational.

A country’s definition of ready will expose capability it does not yet have, not only connections it has not made. That is the point. A gap with a name, an owner and a date is a case the country can act on and a funder can invest in.

MEASURE

Current state against that target, continuously.

A country’s readiness is usually established periodically, by an evaluation that produces a report. What happens between those evaluations is where One Health either operates or does not. The officer who represents a ministry is posted elsewhere and the position sits empty. A joint procedure reaches the date it was due for review and no one reviews it. A laboratory’s approval to run a test expires. A problem found in the last exercise is still not fixed. Each of these is known inside the institution it belongs to, and none of it sits in one place.

This changes the question. Instead of asking whether a country does One Health, the question becomes what the country defined as ready, what is true today, and what remains open.

The readiness view scores current state against the target and tracks the gap over time, continuously. It shows which coordination policies are signed and which are still drafts, whether each ministry’s focal point position is filled, how current the joint procedures are, whether the reference laboratories can confirm what an investigation claims, and when the last exercise was run and its findings closed.

The public and industry interface is scored the same way, so external collaboration is measured, not assumed.

MANAGE

A procedure in a binder is not a capability.

People move. Procedures age. Somebody has to keep the definition current. When either changes, the operating state of the system changes with it.

A procedure trained once, three years ago, when the people in the room have since moved to other posts, is not a capability either. The muscle memory is gone.

A veterinarian and an epidemiologist cannot work out how to work together while a health event is unfolding. That has to happen in a training room, more than once, until it is reflex. Cross-domain training builds the shared language that makes the first hour of a response productive instead of the first week.

The same applies to the positions and the paper. A focal point who transferred in March is a vacancy in the system until someone fills the position, and a procedure that still names the officer who left is out of date whatever the version number says. Both are tracked against the country’s own definition, so the readiness score moves the day a post turns vacant.

Training is scheduled against the roster. Every focal point knows what is current, what is due, and which finding from the last exercise is driving the next session. Not an event. A cycle.

IMPROVE

A gap found in an exercise is a problem solved.

An emergency is the worst possible time to find out which part of the plan does not work. A tabletop exercise reveals in four hours what a health event reveals in four weeks: the phone number that no longer works, the procedure that contradicts another ministry’s, the reporting line nobody defined, the threshold nobody agreed. Finding those in an exercise costs a room and a day. Finding them in an emergency costs lives and credibility.

Exercises run on a schedule across every connected domain. Each one produces findings with named owners and closure dates, and the readiness score reflects whether they were closed. Strategic exercises test the design rather than the drill: whether the right ministries are in the room, whether the law supports the coordination the plan assumes, whether the money moves in hours instead of weeks.

A gap found before the emergency is an opportunity to improve the system.

A gap found in an emergency is a failure that already happened.

An assessment identifies the gap. A strategy sets the direction. Neither one carries the work to closure or shows whether capability actually improved. That is what an operating system does, and it is the difference between a country that documents its intentions and a country that can demonstrate what changed.

THE MISSING PIECE
The country holds the institutions and the mandate. OACI makes the connections between them operable, visible and persistent.

OACI does not replace ministries, laboratories, surveillance systems or national authority, and it does not compete with the surveillance, laboratory and information systems a country already runs or that its partners already fund. It provides the shared operating layer above them, through which the country defines readiness, sees current state, manages the gaps and holds capability over time.

What OACI carries between institutions is the signal and the proof that it is fit to act on: which laboratory can run the method, who is qualified to sign the result, which procedure is current. Where capability is uneven, collaboration is what makes it usable across the country. A district that cannot confirm a result has a route to one that can, and the standing to use it.

This connective layer is the glue: the part that makes existing institutions act as one. OACI connects them. It does not replace them.

The OACI Platform

The platform is not a screen people meet in front of. It runs the collaboration.

It holds the directory of every institution, role and focal point, with an owner accountable for each one being current. It stands up a secure event workgroup in minutes, with the right people already in it, because the roles were assigned before the event. It carries the Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), versioned and assigned, so roles are read rather than argued. It runs the training register and the exercise cycle, and every finding carries a named owner and a closure date until it is closed. Certificates sit with the people who earned them, which is what an accreditation body, a trading partner and a donor all ask for, and what opens the cross-border work they have been kept out of.

Every item on it has an owner and a date. That is the difference between a system that runs and a report that gets read.

Knowledge

Procedures, guidance, case definitions, training content, exercise reports, evaluations, agreements and the decisions taken in meetings are national capability only if the people who need them can find the current version. In most countries that material sits on personal drives, in email and in workshop hard copy. OACI keeps it permissioned, versioned and connected to the roles and the work that depend on it.

When people move, the knowledge stays. When a finding becomes an action, its owner, its date and the evidence of closure stay with it.

Laboratories

A country’s laboratory capability is distributed across institutions and often across several ministries. That makes laboratories one of the clearest tests of whether One Health is actually connected. The country should be able to see which laboratory can perform which test, to which method and at which tier, whether its proficiency results confirm the capability is there, and where a required capability does not yet exist. A country knows what it can test before it needs to test it, not during.

Standards and capability

Coordination is worth what the capability underneath it is worth. Where a country is working toward ISO/IEC 17025 for testing laboratories, ISO 15189 for medical laboratories, or a food safety or inspection standard a trading partner recognises, the requirements, the evidence, the competence records and the open non-conformities are held and tracked continuously rather than assembled before an audit.

That turns accreditation from an event a laboratory prepares for into a state a country can see. It also turns a gap into a plan: a capability the country does not yet have becomes a defined target with an owner, a date and a documented path to reach it.

OACI brings laboratory design, audit and standards expertise alongside the platform, so a country closing a capability gap has both the system that tracks it and the people who have done the work.

Integration

OACI connects through application programming interfaces (APIs) and other authorized interfaces to the national systems a country already operates. Those systems remain authoritative for the data and functions they own. The country decides which systems connect, which signals and indicators are exposed, what triggers collaboration, who may see what, and what flows back.

The signals, indicators and workflow triggers a country authorizes activate the collaboration around them, bringing the right institutions, roles, procedures and work teams together while the underlying national systems stay in place. Because OACI operates across institutional boundaries, it can assemble an authorized cross-domain operating picture that no single ministry system is designed to provide. A signal detected in one system becomes coordinated work across the institutions responsible for acting on it.

Integration moves the signal. OACI moves the work.

As neighbouring countries join, they join the same directory, the same methods and the same procedures, so the first cross-border event is not the first time anyone has met.

The result is a national One Health operating picture without creating another institution or another command center to build, staff and fund.

Every nation keeps control of its own data and its own mandate. A country sends only what it chooses to send: the alerts, indicators, and workflow that coordination needs, never access to the underlying records. Sovereignty is absolute. The country’s systems remain under national control. OACI connects to them through nationally authorized interfaces and exchanges only the information required for the collaboration being performed. Access follows the country’s own permissions, roles and data sharing rules, set by the national data controller. Data sovereignty is a design property of the system, not an assurance offered alongside it.

The platform runs on Government-Standard Security. Encryption to AES-256, multi-factor authentication, role-based access and continuous monitoring, the same infrastructure and standards as FedRAMP United States federal deployments.

Relationships, protocols, institutional knowledge, shared directories, joint exercises and cross-agency communication become permanent capabilities, not temporary crisis scaffolding.

Collaboration is the outcome. Communication, coordination and capacity building are the mechanics that reach it and hold it, every day, across every connected institution, not only in a crisis.

Institutional memory is not a quality an organisation has. It is what remains when the roles, the procedures, the knowledge, the training record and the open findings are held in a system rather than in the people who happen to occupy the posts. When a ministry director changes, the next director inherits that record, not a request to start over.

Programmes end. Funding cycles close. Staff rotate. What a country builds through them either persists in a system it controls or leaves with the people and the projects that carried it.

Those continental strategies call for work across mandates. This is the infrastructure that work depends on. OACI adapts a collaboration system proven over two decades of national operation, and each country shapes it around its own One Health priorities, set by its geography, its current capabilities and its economy.

Government does not see every signal first, and it does not carry the response alone.

Industry often sees a problem in its own product, its own herds or its own supply chain before it reaches a surveillance system. Communities see what is happening where they live before it reaches a clinic. When the response comes, a recall runs through industry, a campaign runs through communities, and a trade decision is carried by exporters. A country that can only work with what its own ministries hold is working with part of the picture and part of the capacity.

Vetted industry, laboratory, academic and community participants are admitted to a specific secure workgroup, for a specific event or policy question, under permissions set by the national data controller. They contribute to the work. They do not gain access to the records underneath it.

Public alerts go out with delivery confirmed and a defined stand-down, not broadcast and assumed. What comes back from the field and the public arrives through structured intake that is acknowledged, triaged and routed to an action, so a report from a district or a citizen becomes work with an owner rather than a message someone read.

Collaboration reaches past the ministry wall.

Access to national records does not.

The United States went through this. A presidential directive in 1997 called for a national integrated food safety system. Six committees followed, and years of meetings, plans, best practices and tools. What did not follow was a system that operated.

That came later, and it started with laboratories. Laboratories under health, agriculture, environment and national defense were connected by role and capability. The country needed to validate methods fast enough to keep pace with the technology. It needed accreditation, so a result from any bench could be trusted. It needed to know where capability and resources actually sat, in every state and nationally. And it needed to know who held every critical position, because those are the people who write the standard operating procedures, set the best practices, and build the education, training and exercises the country’s laboratory capability runs on. A testing inventory held separately in four ministries became a national capability the country could see.

Under a federal cooperative agreement that network grew into the shared operating environment regulators and laboratories have used ever since. Two decades on, it runs as the collaboration system for United States federal health, agriculture and environment agencies and all fifty states. The African implementation is its own system, built on that operating experience. Africa does not have to spend those twenty years again.

No African country funds the build. What each country contributes goes to adapting and running OACI, and every country that joins lowers the cost for the others.

Every country enters One Health through the priorities

Countries do not have to build One Health all at once. A country can begin with the priority it already needs to solve, and each implementation strengthens the same national operating capability.

One Health

Public Health

Food Safety

Food Security

Production, market, climate and plant health signals reaching the institutions that can act while there is still time to act, rather than after a shortfall becomes an emergency.

Environmental Health

Water, air, waste and ecosystem signals connected to the human and animal health institutions that have to respond to them.

Laboratory Networks

Which laboratory runs which test, to which method, at which tier, with proficiency, referral and accreditation status visible across every ministry that depends on it.

Emergency Response

Rosters, procedures, workgroups, exercise cycles and corrective actions held ready before an event, so activation is a step rather than a scramble.

One entry point. A path that does not end.

No single ministry has to carry the full load.

Each entry point belongs to the ministry that already holds the priority, and each can be funded by the agency, partner or programme that already funds that work. What sits underneath is shared, so a country adopting a second solution inherits the directory, the roster and the trained focal points from the first rather than starting again.

The same holds across borders. A solution built once is not rebuilt for every country, and a funder can see where the gap is across the continent rather than funding one country at a time.

Every region inherited institutions built to work apart, and every region is now working to reconnect them. Africa comes to that work with an understanding that reaches deeper than a framework, and it can help lead it.

Championing One Health is not adopting something new. It is reclaiming something that was always Africa's, and giving it infrastructure at the scale it now needs.
