Food Safety

Africa has long understood the chain from soil to table, through the feed and the animals in between, as one. Feed safety is food safety one step earlier: aflatoxin in dairy feed returns as aflatoxin in the milk children drink. Seeing the human and animal sides together is One Health, and it is how a country makes its food safer for everyone who eats it.

In Africa, food safety is a One Health question and a trade question: what reaches the family table, what goes into the feed trough, and what clears the border. Collaboration is how every authority that touches it acts on the same finding.

THE REALITY
Food safety is where One Health and trade meet. The gap sits between the institutions that share the work.

Countries adopt Codex standards and take part in setting them. The African Union Food Safety Strategy for Africa sets a continental direction to 2036, and in February 2025 the African Union adopted the statute of the Africa Food Safety Agency. The African Continental Free Trade Area’s annex on sanitary and phytosanitary measures ties food safety directly to intra-African trade. Heads of Africa’s food regulatory agencies met for the third time in their own forum in 2026. And the International Food Safety Authorities Network, INFOSAN, asks every member country to name an emergency contact point. Each of these depends on a position someone holds today.

The decisions that matter are often taken at someone else’s border. In January 2026 the European Union changed its border checks on food from Africa on the strength of its own laboratory findings: it added strawberries from Egypt for pesticide residues, and halved its checks on Egyptian oranges because their compliance had improved. Either way, the result the exporting country’s institutions have to act on arrives from abroad.

Capacity is uneven, across countries and within them. Some authorities run accredited laboratories and established inspectorates. Others are building them one programme at a time.

A contaminant can start in the soil or the water, pass through feed and an animal, surface in a clinic and stop at a border. Health, agriculture and livestock, veterinary services, the feed regulator, the standards body, trade, customs and local authorities each hold part of it. No single ministry owns it.

That is the gap the OACI Platform is built for: the step between the institutions, which none of them owns and none of them can hold alone.

THE COST
Every part of the cost is counted somewhere. The total is counted nowhere.

The health burden comes first. The African and South-East Asian regions carry the greatest foodborne disease burden, and every published figure is a floor: it counts what reached a clinic. The World Health Organization’s 2026 estimates put the productivity lost to foodborne disease at US$310 billion a year worldwide, and in the African Region it takes 13.66 million years of healthy life every year.

Trade carries the rest: a rejection is usually learned at a foreign border, after the product has shipped. Feed is paid for twice, in the herd and at the table.

Health has the patients. Agriculture has the lost harvest and the feed that cannot be used. Trade has the rejected consignment. The producer has the lost income. What no institution has been assigned is putting them beside each other while the finding is still open.

The OACI Platform is designed to put them beside each other: one finding, every institution that pays part of its cost on it at once, and a record that shows what was done and when.

One Food Supply, Working Together

Food safety is one integrated system, not a set of separate ministries. A finding that starts in a field, a feed mill or a border consignment is acted on by several authorities at once, and it only closes when they have acted on the same finding together. The OACI Platform is built to hold that working space for food and feed as one system: health, agriculture and livestock, the feed regulator, the standards body, trade and customs, and the laboratories and local authorities, each keeping its own mandate.

Every stretch of the chain works the same way. A clinic that sees unexplained illness needs the inspectorate to know what was sold where. A laboratory that finds a residue needs the extension officer and the certifying officer before the next harvest ships. Veterinary services that confirm a problem at an abattoir need health to know before the meat reaches the market. Each institution sees what the others cannot, and none of them is another’s early warning system.

The working space is what makes the join possible. A named position in each authority, one current version of each joint procedure, one alert that reaches every position at once, and a finding that stays open until its owner closes it on evidence. Food and feed move through the same space, because feed is where the chain starts and the milk, meat and eggs are where it ends.

How the United States Already Works Together

The United States has run one integrated food safety system for more than twenty years, and the OACI Platform is built on the proven infrastructure of that system, adapted to Africa’s own institutions. The parts that matter here are the ones where several authorities work one finding together.

Rapid response teams are the clearest example. Multi-agency teams for human and animal food work under one Unified Command, using the Incident Command System, and they are funded through cooperative agreements with states.

The laboratories are shared as well. The separate national laboratory networks, for food, feed, animal health and other hazards, also work together through one integrated consortium of laboratory networks, so a result from one laboratory is trusted by the others. Behind the teams and the laboratories sits one shared, secure place to work. Officials from local, state and federal agencies, and the laboratories and regulators of all fifty states, find one another there, share what they know, exercise together and work an incident from the first signal to its closure.

Because state programmes work to the same program standards and their laboratories to the same methods, an inspection or a result at one level is accepted at the others: the federal agency relies on the state’s work instead of repeating it. That is what makes fifty-one jurisdictions one food safety system.

That shared place is the infrastructure the OACI Platform is built on. For an African country it means the food authority, the feed regulator, veterinary services, standards, trade and the laboratories working in one place, each keeping its own mandate, so that one institution’s finding is one the others can act on.

Working Across Levels

A food safety finding does not stay in one office, and it does not stay at one level of government. It starts at a market, a farm gate or a feed mill, reaches a district office, then a national authority, and then a border. Each level holds part of the answer, and the step between levels is where findings are lost.

The OACI Platform is built to give every level the same working space. A district inspector, a provincial laboratory, a national food authority and a customs post each hold a named position in one directory, and each sees the same current procedure for investigation, trace-back, withdrawal and recall. The work does not need a new system at each level. It needs the positions and the procedures to be the same wherever the finding is.

Working across levels also means working across boundaries of responsibility. Health, agriculture and livestock, the feed regulator, the standards body and trade each answer to a different office. The working space lets each of them act on the finding in its own mandate, at the same time, and close it on evidence, without asking any one office to take over another’s work.

One Health in Practice

One Health is familiar in Africa. It is how communities have long understood the link between people, animals, plants and the environment, and it is a return to that understanding rather than a framework adopted from elsewhere. Food safety is one of the clearest places where it becomes practice: the same food can carry a hazard from the soil, through the feed and the animal, into the clinic and the market.

In practice, One Health in food safety means that the human health authority, the animal health and feed authorities, the plant and environmental authorities and the food standards body act on one finding together. It means the laboratory result that confirms a hazard reaches every authority that must act on it, and that the finding stays open until each of them has done its part.

The OACI Platform gives that practice a place to happen. It does not replace any institution’s authority, and it does not ask any country to adopt a model built elsewhere. It gives African institutions the working space to practise One Health on the food they already manage, and it helps lead the practice for the world.

The Step Between Institutions

Most food safety systems are not short of testing, inspection or regulation. The failure usually comes after the test. A result is produced, a finding is made, and then the next step depends on someone deciding to make a call, knowing who to call, and that person still holding the position.

That step belongs to no single institution. The laboratory does not hold the inspector’s authority, and the inspector does not hold the feed regulator’s. So the step is often left to a call, an email or a meeting, and nobody is accountable for whether it happened.

The OACI Platform is built to make that step a function of the working space itself. Each position that must act is named and current. The alert reaches every position at once. The procedure for the next action is the one every institution has agreed. And the finding stays open, with an owner and a deadline, until it is closed on evidence that every authority involved can read.

Food and Feed in One Chain

Food and feed are one chain. Feed is where the chain starts for most animal-source food, and it is where a hazard is most often first detected and most easily stopped. A mycotoxin in a staple crop, a residue in a feed ingredient, or a contaminant in a feed mill’s water can reach the milk, meat and eggs that families eat, and it can reach them months later.

Today those stretches are often governed by different offices. The feed registration desk sits in one ministry, the food standards body in another, and the laboratory that can confirm the result may be in another city or another country. What the chain needs is one shared picture of the finding, from feed to table, so that each office acts on what it has been told, when it is told.

The OACI Platform does not merge the feed regulator into the food authority, and it does not change which office registers a feed mill. It gives each office the same finding, the same procedure and the same owner, so that a finding that starts in feed is closed by the institutions that hold the feed and the food together.

What a Ministry Can Ask Today

A minister, a programme manager or a technical director can test the working space with a few plain questions. Who holds the inspection position at a given market today, and is that person still qualified? When a residue is confirmed on export produce, does the grower, the certifying officer, the laboratory and trade hear at once? When a border rejection is recorded, who owns the fix, by what date, and what evidence shows it closed?

If the answer to any of these takes more than a day, or depends on one person remembering, the step between institutions is the place to start. A country does not need a new system to ask these questions. It needs the positions, procedures and alerts that answer them to be held in one place, owned by the institutions that already hold the work.

What the OACI Platform Does Not Do

The platform is a working space, not an authority. It does not inspect a market, register a feed mill, certify an exporter or license a laboratory, and it does not decide what a national standard should be. Those decisions stay with the institutions that hold them under national law.

It does not score or rank a country’s food safety system. An assessment is the work of the institution that carries it out, and the platform’s role is to hold the open actions that come out of an assessment, so that they are owned, dated and closed on evidence.

It does not hold the national records. Inspection, laboratory, registration and surveillance records stay on national systems, with the government as the data controller. What moves through the platform is the signal that a finding needs action, and the evidence that the action was taken.

Food Control

Food safety is daily work from the field to the table. All of it depends on who holds each position today and whether they are still qualified to act.

If the answer to who holds a position takes a round of calls and emails, the list was accurate on the day it was drawn up and has not been accountable to anyone since.

PUBLIC HEALTH AND CONSUMER PROTECTION

Foodborne illness is found in clinics and stopped in markets.

Ask who holds the environmental health position for a given market today, and whether the officer inspecting food service this week is still qualified to do it. A cluster of illness at a clinic needs the laboratory that confirms the organism, the inspector who can reach the vendor and the local authority that runs the market.

PRIMARY PRODUCTION AND ANIMAL FEED

Food safety starts where food is grown, stored, fed and slaughtered.

What animals eat becomes the milk, meat and eggs people eat, and feed is often regulated in the agriculture or livestock ministry rather than the food authority. Keeping aflatoxin out of a staple crop and out of dairy feed needs the extension officer, the feed regulator, the standards body that sets the limit, the laboratory that can measure it and the inspector at the mill. Ask who holds the feed registration desk today, and whether the food authority would hear about a feed result before the milk reaches the market.

STANDARDS AND TRADE

A consignment clears a border on the strength of the laboratory behind it.

How does the authority know the officer signing this week's export certificate is still qualified to sign it, or that the laboratory behind the result still passes proficiency for that method? A single residue finding on export produce has to reach the grower, the certifying officer, the laboratory and trade before the next consignment ships.

Much of what Africa exports is audited by private certification bodies before any government inspector sees it. That reliance works only when the regulator can see, today, which certificates are current. The OACI Platform is designed to make that status visible to the regulator, by consent of those who hold it, and certifies, accredits and audits no one.

Why They Are One System

Many mandates, one event. The finding arrives in one institution and the response needs several.

The traffic runs in every direction. A clinic seeing unexplained illness needs the inspectorate to know what has been sold where. A laboratory finding a residue needs the extension officer and the certifying officer before the next harvest ships. A feed result needs the food side before the milk and eggs reach the market. Veterinary services confirming a problem at an abattoir need health to know before the meat reaches the market. None is another's early warning system. Each sees what the others cannot.

What fails is rarely the testing. It is the step after it. Reaching the next institution depends on someone deciding to make a call, knowing who to call, and that person still holding the position. The OACI Platform is built to close that step before the event, while there is still time to change the outcome.

What Collaboration Requires

Collaboration is a working condition, not a relationship.

A memorandum between ministries, a committee that meets each quarter, a workshop that ends in a contact list. All of it is real, and none of it changes what happens on the day an inspector needs an answer from a laboratory she has never worked with.

01
Everyone with a role can be found today.

A directory of positions across every authority in food and feed control, from the district inspector to the certifying officer at the border, each with a named owner.

02
Everyone is working to the same procedure.

One version of each joint procedure for investigation, trace-back, withdrawal and recall, assigned to positions in every authority involved.

03
Everyone hears at once, and it can be shown.

A finding reaches health, agriculture, the feed regulator, veterinary services, standards and trade at the same time, addressed to positions, with delivery confirmed for each recipient.

04
Every finding has an owner and closes with evidence.

A gap from an event, an audit or a border rejection is assigned to a position, carries a deadline, and closes only on evidence every authority involved can read.

Laboratory confirmation sits under all four: proficiency current for the method, accreditation in scope, equipment in service, analyst qualified. Packhouses, feed mills, transporters, certification bodies and private laboratories take part in assigned roles, without access to any national system.

The Missing Piece

What is missing is infrastructure for the collaboration itself.

Food control authorities have built strong work inside each mandate. The work between them still depends on an agreement, a meeting and the people who happen to know one another. No authority can reasonably build that space alone: whoever builds the connection funds it, and the others become guests in a system they do not control.

The OACI Platform is built to give that space a standing collaboration layer around the inspection, registration, laboratory and surveillance systems each authority already runs, food and feed alike, without replacing any of them. What moves through the layer is the signal and the proof that it is fit to act on. A single food safety authority or feed regulator can use it in its own work before a second institution joins. OACI does not assess or score a country.

Collaboration crosses borders. National records never do. Inspection, laboratory, registration, feed and trace-back records stay on national systems, with the government always the data controller. The OACI Platform holds this layer on Government-Standard Security; the OACI Platform page carries the security, sovereignty and integration detail.

One Health is familiar here. OACI is built to give Africa the infrastructure to practise it at the scale of modern institutions, and to help lead it for the world.

Capabilities

Nine conditions carry the work, whatever the finding turns out to be.

DELIVERY CONFIRMED

Did last month's alert actually reach anyone? Findings reach the positions that need them in every authority at once, with delivery confirmed and escalation on a timer.

A CURRENT DIRECTORY

Who holds the inspection position at that market, or the feed registration desk, today? One directory of positions across food and feed control, each with an owner and an alternate.

LABORATORY CONFIRMATION

Which laboratory can confirm it, and will the result stand at a border? Proficiency, accreditation scope, equipment and analyst competence tracked across food, feed, veterinary, clinical and environmental laboratories.

ONE CURRENT VERSION

Is the district using the same trace-back procedure you approved? Joint procedures held once, versioned, owned and acknowledged by every position assigned to them.

COMPETENCE RECORDS

Is the officer signing export certificates still qualified? Programmes issue their own certificates; competencies are tracked across programmes and renewed, with training assigned when a gap calls for it.

CLOSURE ON EVIDENCE

Did the finding from the last border rejection get fixed, and by whom? Every finding assigned to a position, given a deadline and closed only on uploaded evidence.

STANDING RESPONSE TEAMS

When contaminated food or feed is confirmed, is a team stood up, or assembled? Multi-agency teams named in advance by position, trained together and exercised on the procedures they will use for real, each action after an exercise tracked to closure.

CONTINUOUS READINESS

If asked today how ready you are, where does the answer come from? Readiness scored against targets the country sets for itself, each figure dated.

RECORDS THAT STAY

When a director transfers, what does the successor inherit? Procedures, counterparts, decisions and open findings bound to the position, not the person.

Proven at National Scale

The way of working is proven. Africa does not pay to build it again.

For more than twenty years, one collaboration infrastructure has connected human health, animal health, food, environmental and emergency response laboratories and teams across United States federal agencies and all fifty states, co-funded by the food safety, agriculture and homeland security authorities.

In that system food and animal feed are held together: the state and federal officials charged by law with regulating feed work through one association, beside the food side. That is the model the OACI Platform is built on.

OACI does not build Africa's collaboration infrastructure from scratch. It is leveraging the infrastructure proven in the United States: building on it and adapting it to Africa's own institutions, priorities and needs, as an African system, African-owned and African-governed.

No African country funds the build. What each country contributes goes to adapting and running OACI, and every country that joins lowers the cost for the others. OACI is designed so that what works in one country is carried into the next instead of started again. Development partners and funders can support a country's use of OACI instead of commissioning another separate build: the funder keeps the credit, the country keeps the capability, and the investment compounds.

African institutions are already moving the same way on their own: in May 2026 the agriculture ministers of the Southern African Development Community approved a regional Feed and Fodder Strategic Framework. What OACI itself delivers in Africa is recorded as it happens.

How a Country Begins

A country enters through the food safety work that hurts most, and grows in whatever direction it needs.

An export channel at risk. Aflatoxin in a staple or in feed. A laboratory that must pass proficiency to keep a market open. A foodborne cluster nobody could trace. Any of these can anchor the first connection.

Deeper. From national to district, market, abattoir, feed mill and border post. Wider. A second mandate adds its own procedures and findings, not a second directory. Across. The same mandate in the country next door, so a trade corridor has counterpart positions and a cross-border finding closes with evidence in both.

What a country commits to at the start is small: a named position accountable for the directory, a first set of procedures and a date for the first exercise. The first year is a measurement, not a verdict.

What a food safety authority gains is the part of its work it cannot discharge alone. The commitments it already carries, to INFOSAN, the Africa Food Safety Agency, the African Continental Free Trade Area and its own national plan, all require other institutions to act on its findings. That is the work the OACI Platform is built to hold.

Each authority keeps its own mandate, its own systems and its own budget. A briefing starts with where food safety work is breaking down now, in whichever authority is carrying it.

The chain from soil to table was always understood as one. The OACI Platform is built to give the institutions that guard it, food and feed, one place to work together.
