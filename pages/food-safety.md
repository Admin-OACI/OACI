Food Safety

Food safety collaboration at national scale has been done before. For twenty-five years it has run across every level of a national food safety system, from federal agencies to the laboratories and inspectors in every state. OACI brings that working system to Africa, and each country adapts it around what its own food safety authorities have already built.

In Africa, food safety is a One Health question and a trade question: what reaches the family table, and what clears the border. Collaboration is how every authority that touches it acts on the same finding.

PROVEN AT NATIONAL SCALE
Africa’s food safety strategy stands where another nation stood in 1997. What comes next already exists.

In 1997 a United States presidential directive called for a national food safety system. Committees, plans and workshops followed, as they have followed the African Union Food Safety Strategy for Africa. The operating system came later, and it began with laboratories: a directory built so laboratories could find one another became the working environment regulators and laboratories shared. When the Food Safety Modernization Act of 2011 wrote an integrated national food safety system into law, the infrastructure to carry it was already operational and being deployed nationally, co-funded by the Food and Drug Administration, the Department of Agriculture and the Department of Homeland Security.

Over twenty-five years that collaboration infrastructure has grown to connect federal food safety, agriculture and homeland security agencies with the laboratories and regulators of all fifty states, through FoodSHIELD, the Food Emergency Response Network, the National Animal Health Laboratory Network and the Integrated Consortium of Laboratory Networks. It was shaped through real events and real corrective actions by the officials who had to work together through them.

Rapid response teams changed how food emergencies are handled. After the 2006 E. coli event linked to fresh spinach, federal and state investigators were still running parallel, separate investigations, duplicating effort without shared procedures. In 2008 the Food and Drug Administration began funding nine pilot states to build standing, multi-agency teams of federal, state and local regulators, epidemiologists and laboratories, working under a unified command structure agreed before any event, with one aim: shorten the time between notification of a contamination event and effective control. Each team works in its own dedicated portal on the same collaboration technology OACI brings to Africa, with members named in advance, plans and procedures held in one place, and joint training and exercises. Each starts from a capability assessment that names its gaps. After every incident or exercise an after-action report and improvement plan follow, and every action item is tracked to closure. Defined from the start as all-hazards, for food and feed, the program became the model the Food and Drug Administration used to extend federal and state reliance under the Food Safety Modernization Act. Today thirty teams work through their own portals on that technology, and the teams outside the funded program show what the infrastructure carries on its own: standing teams, exercised, measured and improving, without a federal grant behind them.

For Africa the parallel reaches beyond food. Many countries already train public health rapid response teams through integrated disease surveillance and response. What the United States teams added was the standing structure around them: members from several agencies named in advance, one place for plans and procedures, a capability assessment, and every after-action finding tracked to closure. On OACI that structure serves every solution. One standing, cross-domain team is defined once, and at the event the domains it needs activate, whether the finding starts in food, in animals, in people or in the environment.

Those years, and the public investment behind them, are already spent. No African country is asked to spend them again. OACI adapts that record for Africa, and each country shapes it around its own priorities.

OACI began with an African call for this system.

In July 2022, Lucia Anelich of South Africa opened the annual meeting of the International Association for Food Protection with the Ivan Parkin Lecture, “Out of Africa.” She called for harmonised food safety standards across the continent, and pointed to the United States Food Safety Modernization Act as the ideal model: a law that calls for one integrated food safety system, connecting federal, state and local authorities across all fifty states. The idea grew through South Africa’s food safety summit in 2023, laboratory and standards leaders, and the African Union Food Safety Strategy for 2022 to 2036.

In August 2024, Botswana’s first food safety symposium brought together ministries, standards organizations, the African Union, laboratories and field services, and agreed it had to be built. When the approach was presented to African Union member states later that year, participants recognised it as One Health in practice.

From fifty states to fifty-five nations, it began in food safety. Born in Africa, OACI is governed by the African institutions it serves.

THE REALITY
Food safety is where One Health and trade meet. The gap sits between the institutions that share the work.

Countries adopt Codex standards and take part in setting them. The African Union Food Safety Strategy for Africa sets a continental direction to 2036. National food control laws assign responsibility, and laboratories, inspectorates and standards bodies carry it out every day.

The continental machinery is in place too. The African Continental Free Trade Area’s annex on sanitary and phytosanitary measures ties food safety directly to intra-African trade. The African Organisation for Standardisation harmonises standards across its member states. Heads of food regulatory agencies from 37 African Union member states held the first African Food Regulatory Authorities Forum in 2023. And the International Food Safety Authorities Network, INFOSAN, asks every member country to name an emergency contact point. Each of these depends on a position someone holds today.

Capacity is uneven, across countries and within them. Some authorities run accredited laboratories and established inspectorates. Others are building them one programme at a time.

Food safety is where all four One Health domains meet on one event. A contaminant can start in the soil or the water, pass through an animal, surface in a clinic and stop at a border. Health, agriculture, veterinary services, the standards body, trade, customs and local authorities each hold part of it. No single ministry owns it.

Food safety reaches further than any ministry’s map. Water used for irrigation, washing and processing. Power that keeps a cold room cold through an outage. Transport that decides how many hours produce spends on a road or at a border. Municipal markets, customs, tourism and security. None of these is a food safety authority, and each can decide whether food arrives safe.

A contaminated product does not announce whether it was an accident, fraud or a deliberate attack, and in the first days nobody knows which. The investigation needs the same people, procedures and lines of communication whatever the cause, including security services working as a peer. Food safety and food defence together are food protection, and they run on one infrastructure.

THE COST
Every part of the cost is counted somewhere. The total is counted nowhere.

Food control answers to a clear benchmark. The FAO/WHO Food Control System Assessment Tool sets out what a national system should be able to do, and the AU strategy commits countries to building toward it.

The health burden comes first. WHO’s 2015 estimates put foodborne disease in the African Region at more than 91 million illnesses and 137,000 deaths a year, the highest burden per person of any region, and every published figure is a floor. The World Bank’s 2019 Safe Food Imperative puts the productivity lost in Africa at US$16.7 billion a year, before the cost of treatment. Trade carries the rest: one contaminated consignment can close an export channel for a season, and a rejection is usually learned at a foreign border, after the product has shipped.

Health has the patients. Agriculture has the lost harvest. Trade has the rejected consignment. The producer has the lost income. What no institution has been assigned is putting them beside each other while the finding is still open.

Ask what the last food safety event cost the country, and the honest answer is that nobody added it up.

FOOD CONTROL
Food safety is daily work from the field to the table. All of it depends on who holds each position today and whether they are still qualified to act.

Every one of these channels has positions behind it. If the answer to who holds one takes a round of phone calls, the list was accurate on the day it was drawn up and has not been accountable to anyone since.

PUBLIC HEALTH AND CONSUMER PROTECTION
Foodborne illness is found in clinics and stopped in markets.

Foodborne illness surveillance and investigation. Hygiene inspection of food service, markets and street vendors. Water safety where food is prepared. Consumer complaints. Education for handlers and consumers.

Ask who holds the environmental health position for a given market today, and whether the officer inspecting food service this week is still certified to do it.

A cluster of illness at a clinic needs the laboratory that confirms the organism, the inspector who can reach the vendor and the local authority that runs the market.

PRIMARY PRODUCTION
Food safety starts where food is grown, stored and slaughtered.

Good agricultural practice, pesticide control and safe storage. Feed safety. Inspection of abattoirs and food of animal origin. Residue and antimicrobial use monitoring. Extension advice at the source.

Ask who holds the meat inspection position at a given abattoir today, and whether the inspector signing off carcasses this week is still qualified.

Keeping aflatoxin out of a staple crop needs the extension officer who advises on drying and storage, the standards body that sets the limit, the laboratory that can measure it, the inspector at the mill, and the trade authority that answers when a neighbouring country tests the grain.

STANDARDS AND TRADE
A consignment clears a border on the strength of the laboratory behind it.

Setting and adopting national standards and taking part in Codex. Registration and licensing of establishments. Import control. Export certification. Laboratory accreditation and proficiency.

How does the authority know the officer signing this week’s export certificate is still qualified to sign it, or that the laboratory behind the result still passes proficiency for that method?

A single residue finding on export produce has to reach the grower, the certifying officer, the laboratory and trade before the next consignment ships.

WHY THEY ARE ONE SYSTEM
Many mandates, one event. The finding arrives in one institution and the response needs several.

The traffic runs in every direction. A clinic seeing unexplained illness needs the inspectorate to know what has been sold where. A laboratory finding a residue needs the extension officer and the certifying officer before the next harvest ships. Veterinary services confirming a problem at an abattoir need health to know before the meat reaches the market. None is another’s early warning system. Each sees what the others cannot.

What fails is rarely the testing. It is the step after it. Reaching the next institution depends on someone deciding to make a call, knowing who to call, and that person still holding the position. The connection that closes that step has to exist before the event, while there is still time to change the outcome.

WHAT COLLABORATION REQUIRES
Collaboration is a working condition, not a relationship.

A memorandum between ministries, a committee that meets each quarter, a workshop that ends in a contact list. All of it is real, and none of it changes what happens on the day an inspector needs an answer from a laboratory she has never worked with.

01
Everyone with a role can be found today.

A directory of positions across every authority in food control, from the district inspector to the certifying officer at the border, each with a named owner. When a position falls vacant, the system knows the same day.

02
Everyone is working to the same procedure.

One version of each joint procedure for investigation, trace-back, withdrawal and recall, assigned to positions in every authority involved, with an owner, a review date and an audit trail.

03
Everyone hears at once, and it can be shown.

A finding reaches health, agriculture, veterinary services, standards and trade at the same time, addressed to positions, across email, SMS and voice, with delivery confirmed for each recipient.

04
Every finding has an owner and closes with evidence.

A gap from an event, an audit or a border rejection is assigned to a position, carries a deadline, and closes only on evidence every authority involved can read.

Laboratory confirmation sits under all four. A result clears a border when the laboratory behind it is trusted: proficiency current for the method, accreditation in scope, equipment in service, analyst qualified. No single institution holds that assurance for the others.

Many of the people in the work are not civil servants. Packhouses, transporters, producer associations, private laboratories and methods bodies each hold part of what an event requires. They take part in assigned roles, without access to any national system.

When those four hold, an event does not begin with a scramble.

THE MISSING PIECE
What is missing is infrastructure for the collaboration itself.

Food control authorities have built strong work inside each mandate. The work between them still depends on an agreement, a meeting and the people who happen to know one another.

No authority can reasonably build that space alone. Whoever builds the connection funds it, and the others become guests in a system they do not control. An appropriation is scoped to a mandate. A grant has a host, a scope and an end date.

The OACI Platform gives that space a standing collaboration layer without replacing the systems beneath it. Inspection, laboratory, registration and surveillance records stay on national systems. What moves through the layer is the signal and the proof that it is fit to act on.

OACI does not assess or score a country. It holds the work of closing what the assessment found: the discipline laboratories know from accreditation, applied to the work between institutions. A single food safety authority can use it in its own work before a second institution joins.

Collaboration crosses borders. National records never do.

Establishment registries and trace-back data stay on national systems, with the government always the data controller.

The OACI Platform holds this layer on Government-Standard Security, with the government always the data controller. The OACI Platform page carries the security, sovereignty and integration detail.

No African country funds the build. What a country contributes goes to adapting and running it, and every country that joins lowers the cost for the others.

CAPABILITIES
Nine conditions carry the work, whatever the finding turns out to be.
DELIVERY CONFIRMED
Did last month’s alert actually reach anyone?

Findings reach the positions that need them in every authority at once, with delivery confirmed and escalation on a timer.

A CURRENT DIRECTORY
Who holds the inspection position at that market, today?

One directory of positions across food control, each with an owner and an alternate, validated at a rhythm each institution sets.

LABORATORY CONFIRMATION
Which laboratory can confirm it, and will the result stand at a border?

Proficiency, accreditation scope, equipment and analyst certification tracked across food, veterinary, clinical and environmental laboratories.

ONE CURRENT VERSION
Is the district using the same trace-back procedure you approved?

Joint procedures held once, versioned, owned and acknowledged by every position assigned to them.

COMPETENCE RECORDS
Is the officer signing export certificates still qualified?

Certificates issued, tracked and renewed, with training assigned when a gap calls for it, wherever the training is delivered.

CLOSURE ON EVIDENCE
Did the finding from the last border rejection get fixed, and by whom?

Every finding assigned to a position, given a deadline and closed only on uploaded evidence. The next exercise tests whether the fix held.

STANDING RESPONSE TEAMS
When contaminated food is confirmed, is a team stood up, or assembled?

Multi-agency teams drawn from health, agriculture, veterinary services, standards and the laboratories that serve them, named in advance by position, trained together and exercised on the procedures they will use for real. Each team works under a unified command structure agreed before the event and activates the domains the event requires. It starts from a capability assessment that names its gaps. After every activation or exercise, an after-action report and improvement plan follow, each action with an owner and a deadline, re-tested in the next exercise.

CONTINUOUS READINESS
If asked today how ready you are, where does the answer come from?

Readiness scored against targets the country sets for itself, each figure dated, lapses flagged before an event finds them.

RECORDS THAT STAY
When a director transfers, what does the successor inherit?

Procedures, counterparts, decisions and open findings bound to the position, not the person.

HOW A COUNTRY BEGINS
A country enters through the food safety work that hurts most, and grows in whatever direction it needs.

An export channel at risk. Aflatoxin in a staple. A laboratory that must pass proficiency to keep a market open. A foodborne cluster nobody could trace. Any of these can anchor the first connection.

Deeper.

From national to district, market, abattoir and border post. The same directory, procedures and closure cycle where the work happens.

Wider.

Other entry points open on the same platform. A second mandate adds its own procedures and findings, not a second directory. The standing response team a country builds for its first entry point is the same team every later solution activates, with new domains joining as their positions are filled.

Across.

The same mandate in the country next door, so a trade corridor has counterpart positions and a cross-border finding closes with evidence in both.

What a country commits to at the start is small: a named position accountable for the directory, a first set of procedures and a date for the first exercise. The first year is a measurement, not a verdict.

The chain from soil to table was always understood as one.
OACI gives the institutions that guard it one place to work together.

What a food safety authority gains is the part of its work it cannot discharge alone. The commitments it already carries, to INFOSAN, the Africa Food Safety Agency, the African Continental Free Trade Area and its own national plan, all require other institutions to act on its findings. That is the work OACI holds. In the first year the test is practical: positions named and current, joint procedures agreed and exercised, readiness read against the country’s own plan. The layer outlasts grants and staff changes.

Each authority keeps its own mandate, its own systems and its own budget. A briefing starts with where food safety work is breaking down now, in whichever authority is carrying it.
