THE REALITY
Africa's hunger is watched closely. Acting on what is seen is where time is lost.

Africa runs serious food security analysis: national early warning units, IPC and Cadre Harmonisé analysis cells, and regional systems across the Sahel, the Horn and Southern Africa. The classification is rigorous. The distance between a classification and a coordinated decision across ministries, partners and borders is where families wait. And when a programme ends, the teams, practices and training it built usually end with it.

Four dimensions. Four domains. One outcome.

Food safety asks whether food will harm the people who eat it. Food security asks whether families can count on enough of it, every season, and whether it nourishes their children. No single ministry holds that whole answer.

Availability

Plant and animal health

Crops, livestock, pests and transboundary animal disease decide what is produced and what survives to harvest.

Access

Trade, markets, social protection

Prices, market flows and cross-border trade decide whether households can reach what is produced.

Utilization

Human health and nutrition

Diet quality, safe food, clean water and child feeding decide whether food becomes growth.

Stability

Environment and disaster management

Drought, flood, climate cycles and conflict decide whether the other three hold through a shock.

THE COST
Well funded, rarely sustained. Every restart is paid for twice.

Financing for food sectors in crisis contexts has fallen back to levels last seen nearly a decade ago, while acute food insecurity has stayed high. When budgets contract, duplication and restarts stop being inefficiencies. They become response that does not happen.

Late response. Action taken after a classification escalates costs more and reaches fewer families than action taken on the warning.

Parallel programmes. Partners fund the same goals in the same geography without seeing each other's work.

Repeated training. Capacity that disperses at grant end is paid for again by the next grant.

Relearned lessons. What worked in the last drought response is rebuilt from memory, if at all.

Research that stays on the shelf. Proven methods for production, distribution and preservation reach one country's project and stop there.

THE MISSING PIECE
Sustained collaboration infrastructure, so what works keeps working.

OACI is not another analysis system or another programme. It is the permanent layer between institutions: the teams, the practices, the training and the record of what worked, owned by the country and co-funded across the continent. National early warning units, analysis cells and research centres keep doing what they do best. OACI is where the people who act on their work collaborate, season after season.

Before there were grants, there were granaries.

African communities built food security together, stored against hard years, and passed on what worked. OACI rebuilds that at the scale of a nation and a continent.

PROVEN AT NATIONAL SCALE
The mechanics food security needs have run a national system for more than twenty years.

The infrastructure behind OACI has carried a national integrated food system across all 50 US states for over two decades: federal, state and local agriculture and health agencies, laboratories, academia and industry, each using it for its own mandate. What that record proved was never the directory or the alert. It was the loop: gaps found, owned, fixed and retested, year after year. Food security asks those same mechanics to serve a different set of institutions around a different question. Africa starts with the original vision, every institution using it for its own purposes.

ENTRY POINT
Start with one warning and the people who act on it.

A country does not need to bring its whole food system on at once. It starts with one seasonal risk it already watches, the positions across ministries that must act on it, and one exercised pathway from warning to decision, convened by the body that already holds the mandate: a national food and nutrition security council, the CAADP focal point, or the ministry of agriculture working with health, trade and disaster management.

STEP 1

Map the positions

Who in each ministry, district and partner holds the decision for this risk.

STEP 2

Validate the directory

Each institution chooses how its positions are confirmed and how often.

STEP 3

Exercise the pathway

Run the warning through the workspace before the season, with every domain that owns a part.

STEP 4

Close the gaps

Each gap named, owned and dated. Where competence is the gap, training runs. The next exercise tests whether it held.

What the first deployment delivers

SCOPE

One seasonal risk, the ministries and districts that act on it, and their partners.

TIME

A first exercised pathway within one growing season.

OACI PROVIDES

Deployment, role identification with the country, directory set-up and validation, the shared workspace, training and exercise facilitation, and the after-action record.

THE COUNTRY PROVIDES

What the country gets: a baseline of its own readiness, a named list of gaps with owners and dates, and a working pathway it can run again next season.
FINANCING
Built to fit financing a country already has.

A food security deployment can sit inside a component of a national CAADP agrifood systems investment plan, a GAFSP country-led proposal, development bank financing or the national budget. Operation continues through a yearly subscription, and the cost per country falls as more countries join.
